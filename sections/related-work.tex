\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Architecture Erosion and Reconstruction}

Architecture erosion denotes progressive misalignment between intended architecture and implementation. De Silva and Balasubramaniam organize erosion-control work into strategies that minimize, prevent, or repair erosion and argue that no single strategy is sufficient \cite{desilva2012erosion}. A later systematic mapping study covering 73 primary studies reports that erosion manifests through architectural violations and structural problems and also affects software quality and evolution \cite{li2022erosion}. These findings motivate continuous, implementation-linked checks while cautioning against reducing architecture quality to a single structural metric.

Software architecture reconstruction addresses the related problem of recovering architectural views from implementation artifacts. Ducasse and Pollet distinguish bottom-up, top-down, and hybrid reconstruction processes \cite{ducasse2009reconstruction}. ArchSync is narrower than general reconstruction: it starts from an explicit expected model and extracts only a defined set of service and infrastructure relationships needed for conformance decisions.

\subsection{Static Architecture Conformance}

Software Reflexion Models compare a high-level model with a source-derived model and identify convergence, divergence, and absence \cite{murphy1995reflexion}. Knodel and Popescu compare reflexion models, relation conformance rules, and component access rules across 13 dimensions, showing that static conformance has multiple useful but non-equivalent formulations \cite{knodel2007comparison}. Dependency Constraint Language (DCL) expresses allowed and forbidden dependencies for object-oriented systems and statically detects silent architectural erosion \cite{terra2009dcl}.

ArchSync inherits the central expected-versus-implemented comparison from this line of work. Its prototype-specific contribution is an operational combination for TypeScript service topology: typed HTTP, data, and asynchronous edges; explicit require and require-path semantics; source and model evidence in a versioned finding contract; and separate violation and evolution outcomes. The work does not claim that expected-observed graph comparison or dependency constraints are new.

\subsection{Conformance in Continuous Integration}

Pinto et al. integrate DCL-based conformance into Jenkins and report an industrial-style Java evaluation in which 44 constraints were introduced across six releases and 42 detected violations were corrected before the following release \cite{pinto2017archci}. This demonstrates the value of moving conformance into the integration loop. ArchSync similarly exposes deterministic findings as GitHub annotations and exit-code merge decisions. Its evaluation remains narrower: it tests change-scoped correctness, cache behavior, and replay on a controlled benchmark, not adoption, developer behavior, or organizational effectiveness.

\subsection{Evaluation and Static-Analysis Reliability}

Empirical rigor and artifact availability are recurring concerns in software-architecture research. Konersmann et al. classify evaluation and replication support in 153 ICSA and ECSA papers and argue that improved repeatability and generalizability would strengthen the field \cite{konersmann2022replicability}. In adjacent microservice-recovery research, Abgaz et al. report that standardized datasets, metrics, and baselines remain scarce and that bespoke evaluations limit comparison \cite{abgaz2023decomposition}. These findings motivate ArchSync's executable manifests, pinned runtime artifacts, explicit denominators, and cross-platform replay, but they do not make a synthetic development benchmark representative.

False positives and false negatives are also central to static-analysis reliability. Kaindlstorfer et al. distinguish soundness issues that can yield false negatives from precision issues that can yield false positives. Their peer-reviewed ASE 2024 study tested eight mature analyzers and found 24 unique issues, including 16 soundness issues \cite{kaindlstorfer2024interrogation}. ArchSync therefore complements end-to-end topology changes with annotated positive and hard-negative source signals. The challenge corpus is intentionally a regression instrument: it tests method-name lookalikes, unused imports, aliases, namespace imports, and previously unsupported operations, but is not an independent holdout.

\subsection{Related-Work Review Protocol and Current Status}

The preceding synthesis positions ArchSync against selected foundational, secondary, and empirical studies. It is not the outcome of a completed systematic literature review and makes no claim that the relevant literature has been exhaustively identified. A separate versioned protocol follows the software-engineering review process of Kitchenham and Charters \cite{kitchenham2007slr}, supplements database search with backward and forward snowballing \cite{wohlin2014snowballing}, and plans transparent reporting with PRISMA 2020 and the software-engineering-specific SEGRESS guidance \cite{page2021prisma,kitchenham2023segress}.

The candidate protocol predeclares six secondary questions, IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, OpenAlex, and Semantic Scholar, three query families, a fixed 16 August 2026 search cutoff, sentinel recall checks, eligibility and deduplication rules, independent title/abstract and full-text screening, quality assessment, extraction, and synthesis. OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar were selected as accessible cross-publisher indexes with reproducible API requests and paginated exports; they are not treated as coverage-equivalent replacements for Scopus or the Web of Science Core Collection. A separate reference-quality policy requires verified publication identity, prefers papers from 2022--2026, gives journal Q1 first priority and treats Q1/Q2 as high-ranked, and requires a documented foundational exception for older work. Rapid Journal Quality Check supports the initial venue check, but the retained evidence must identify the underlying ranking source, year, and category. Recency and quartile affect citation priority and evidential weight; they do not exclude records from the systematic review. The SLR-102 query specification version \texttt{0.2.0} decomposes six keyword groups into six logical queries, supplies database-specific field and filter translations, and predeclares 24 execution-log rows. The SLR-103 screening codebook version \texttt{0.1.0} defines eight inclusion criteria, ten controlled exclusion reasons, primary-reason precedence, dual-review decision records, and an independent calibration gate. The SLR-104 literature matrix schema version \texttt{0.2.0} defines publication identity, DOI/URL, method, system, language, dataset, evidence source, metric, limitation, relevance, claim/RQ links, exact source locations, two-person verification, and record-hash provenance. It contains zero publication rows while upstream review gates remain blocked and therefore does not constitute literature evidence. The review policy permits members to delegate authorized browser, database, artifact-generation, and validation operations to AI. AI-generated assertions are not evidence, but official output captured by AI can enter the evidence bundle when its source, query, time, retained artifact, and hash are preserved and a named human verifies and accepts the exact bundle. The SLR-102 and SLR-103 templates contain no execution timestamp, result count, screening decision, or export hash. The SLR-104 matrix contains no publication extraction. These artifacts are versioned planning contracts rather than review results. At this manuscript snapshot, protocol version \texttt{0.2.0} is a review candidate. The official search has not started, and no result list has been inspected. Search remains blocked until a non-author reviewer validates the method and sentinel recall and the protocol is frozen. Consequently, this paper reports no search count, study-selection count, PRISMA flow, or literature-derived coverage claim.
